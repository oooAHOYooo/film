Film Grammar

Rules & conventions — Creatures in the Tall Grass

Axis & Continuity

180-Degree Rule
Establish an axis of action between subjects and keep the camera on one side of it throughout a scene. Crossing the line reverses screen direction and disorients the viewer.
Exception: a motivated camera move that crosses the line mid-shot resets the axis and is acceptable.
30-Degree Rule
When cutting between two shots of the same subject, the camera angle must shift by at least 30 degrees. Smaller jumps create a jarring jump cut rather than a clean edit.
Screen Direction
A subject moving left-to-right in one shot must continue moving left-to-right in the next unless a neutral shot (facing camera, stationary, or cutaway) separates them.
Eyeline Match
When a character looks off-screen, the next shot shows what they see from an angle and height consistent with their position and gaze. Mismatched eyelines break the illusion of shared space.
Match on Action
Cut mid-action so the motion continues seamlessly across the edit — e.g., cut as a hand reaches for a door handle, completing the reach in the next shot. Keeps momentum through cuts.

Shot Composition

Rule of Thirds
Divide the frame into a 3×3 grid. Place subjects and key elements along the grid lines or at their intersections rather than dead center. Creates visual tension and leads the eye naturally.
Lead Room / Looking Room
Leave space in the direction a subject is moving or looking. A figure walking right needs open space on the right side of frame; a figure looking left needs space on the left.
Headroom
Maintain a small, consistent gap between the top of a subject's head and the top of frame. Too much headroom feels accidental; too little feels claustrophobic unless intentional.
Shot Scale Hierarchy
Progress shots from wide (establishing context) to tighter (focusing emotion). Establish, Medium, Close-up. Reversing the order without motivation disjoints pacing.
Background Separation
Ensure subjects don't visually merge with background elements (poles "growing" from heads, horizon lines bisecting necks). Use depth, angle, or lens choice to separate planes.

Camera Movement

Movement Must Be Motivated
Every camera move should have a narrative or emotional reason — following a subject, revealing new information, expressing a character's internal state. Unmotivated movement draws attention to itself.
Start and End on a Hold
Begin a pan, tilt, or dolly from a static frame and settle back to a static frame at the end. This gives editors clean in and out points and gives the viewer an anchor.
Zoom vs. Dolly
A zoom compresses perspective; a dolly move through space preserves it. Dollying in creates a sense of presence and depth. Zooming in is faster but feels different — choose deliberately, not by default.
Handheld Energy
Handheld implies immediacy, anxiety, or documentary realism. It should be consistent within a scene's tone — switching between locked and handheld mid-scene signals a deliberate shift in perspective or emotion.

Editing Principles

Cut on Motivation, Not Time
Hold a shot until there is a reason to cut — the action resolves, new information is needed, or the emotion shifts. Cutting before the moment deflates it; cutting after loses energy.
Favor the Reaction
Often the most powerful image is not the action but the character's face responding to it. Cut to reaction as soon as the story beat lands.
L-Cut and J-Cut
Audio can lead or trail the picture cut. An L-cut lets audio from shot A continue into shot B; a J-cut brings audio from shot B in before the picture switches. Both smooth transitions and link scenes.
Parallel Editing Implies Simultaneity
Cutting between two separate locations tells the audience both events are happening at the same time. Don't use parallel cutting for events that are not concurrent without a clear temporal marker.

Sound

Diegetic vs. Non-Diegetic
Diegetic sound exists in the story world (characters can hear it). Non-diegetic sound is added for the audience (score, voiceover). Mixing them without clear intent creates ambiguity about the film's reality.
Room Tone
Record at least 30 seconds of room tone (ambient silence) at every location. It fills gaps in the edit and maintains audio continuity across cuts.
Dialogue Priority
In most narrative scenes, dialogue intelligibility takes precedence over ambient realism. The audience must be able to hear the words; everything else supports that.

Found-Footage Specific

Camera Operator Logic
Every shot must be explainable by who is holding the camera and why. If no in-world cameraperson is established, the shot shouldn't exist. Maintain consistent in-universe camera identity throughout a scene.
Imperfect Framing is Intentional
Reframes, slight loss of focus, and amateur compositions are features of the found-footage register — but they must read as deliberate craft, not actual mistakes. Motivated sloppiness differs from uncontrolled sloppiness.
Continuity of Recording Reason
The audience asks: why is this still being recorded? Keep the narrative logic of recording behavior consistent. Characters stopping and starting the camera should have reasons visible in the story.
Restricted Information
The camera can only know what the in-world operator knows. Resist cutting to angles or information that would be impossible given the single diegetic camera conceit.
